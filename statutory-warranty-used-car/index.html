Statutory warranty on a used car: what each state gives you, and what it doesn't

Every state makes a licensed dealer stand behind a used car for a short period. The period is short, the exclusions are long, and private sales get nothing. Here is the rule in each state, in one table, and what an extended warranty adds on top.

A statutory warranty (NSW calls it a dealer guarantee) is a repair obligation the law puts on a licensed motor dealer when they sell a used car that is young enough and has done few enough kilometres. The dealer must fix covered defects so the car is in reasonable condition for its age. It is not insurance, it does not pay you money, and it is over in about three months. Everything after that rests on the Australian Consumer Law and, if you buy one, an extended warranty.

The rule in each state

The dealer's obligation depends on the car's age and odometer on the day of sale, and in WA and SA on the price as well. "Whichever comes first" means a car driven 5,000 km in six weeks is out of warranty in six weeks.

Tasmania and the Northern Territory run their own schemes with different thresholds; check Consumer, Building and Occupational Services (Tasmania) or NT Consumer Affairs before relying on a figure. In every state, commercial vehicles, most motorcycles and cars sold at auction with a no-warranty notice are outside the scheme, and the Australian Consumer Law still applies to any dealer sale.

What the statutory warranty covers

Defects that stop the car being in reasonable condition for its age and kilometres, repaired by the dealer at the dealer's cost. The common exclusions across the states: tyres and batteries, globes, paint and upholstery, radios and navigation, accessories you fitted, accident or misuse after delivery, and anything the dealer listed on a defect notice at the time of sale. That last one matters: in Victoria and NSW the dealer can write known faults onto the contract and exclude them, as long as the car is still safe, and buyers sign it without reading. The repair time is added to the warranty period, and arranging your own repair without the dealer's agreement can void it.

Private sales: nothing

No state gives a statutory warranty on a private sale, and the Australian Consumer Law guarantees apply to businesses, not to a person selling their own car. If the gearbox fails the week after you buy privately, that is your gearbox. The two things that change the picture are an independent pre-purchase inspection before you pay, and an extended warranty after. The warranty needs a vehicle condition report from a licensed mechanic before it can be lodged, so the inspection does both jobs; how cover works after a private purchase.

After the statutory warranty ends

For a dealer sale, the Australian Consumer Law guarantee of acceptable quality continues past the statutory period, and for a major failure it can mean a repair, refund or replacement. The catch is that "acceptable" is judged against the car's age, price and kilometres, the dealer decides whether to agree, and if they don't, you are in a tribunal arguing it. That works for a 2-year-old car with a failed engine; it rarely works for an 11-year-old car with a failed air-conditioning compressor. An extended warranty replaces that argument with a contract: a listed set of covered parts, a dollar cap per claim, and a claims line that pays the repairer.
