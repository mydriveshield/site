Used car warranty in Victoria: the dealer statutory warranty, and what covers you after it

What a licensed VIC dealer must repair, for how long, what is excluded, why a private sale gets nothing, and what an Integrity extended warranty costs for a car bought in Melbourne, Geelong, Ballarat, Bendigo and regional Victoria.

Buy a used car from a licensed dealer in Victoria and the Motor Car Traders Act 1986 makes the dealer repair covered defects for a short period after sale. That period is the statutory warranty. It is short, it has exclusions, it does not exist on a private sale, and once it ends you are relying on the Australian Consumer Law or an extended warranty. The VIC rule is below, with the government source, then what an extended warranty costs on top.

The VIC rule

A licensed Victorian motor car trader must give a statutory warranty on a used car that is not more than 10 years old (by the build plate) and has travelled under 160,000 km: 3 months or 5,000 km, whichever comes first. Motorcycles, commercial vehicles and cars sold at public auction are exempt. Source: Consumer Affairs Victoria. The dealer repairs covered defects so the car is in reasonable condition for its age and kilometres; it is not a new-car warranty and it pays you no money.

Cars outside the rule

A used car over 10 years old or over 160,000 km gets no statutory warranty in Victoria. Only the Australian Consumer Law guarantees apply. The national guide has every state's thresholds side by side.

What is excluded

Tyres and batteries, globes and sealed-beam lights, audio and navigation, non-standard alarms and keyless entry, power outlets, clocks, most tools, accidental damage or misuse after delivery, and any fault the trader listed on the defect notice with a repair estimate. The trader must pay towing if a warranty defect stops the car being driven, but does not have to supply a courtesy car.

Private sales in VIC

None of this applies to a private sale. The statutory warranty is a dealer obligation, and the Australian Consumer Law guarantees cover businesses, not a person selling their own car. On a private sale in VIC the protection is what you do before you pay: an independent pre-purchase inspection, a PPSR check, and the state's transfer paperwork. An Integrity warranty can be bought on a privately purchased car once a licensed mechanic's condition report is done and is clear of existing faults; how that works.

After the statutory warranty ends

For a dealer sale, the Australian Consumer Law guarantee of acceptable quality continues, judged against the car's age, price and kilometres, and enforced by you against the dealer, in a tribunal if it comes to that. For an older car that argument is weak. An extended warranty is a contract instead: a listed set of covered parts, a dollar cap per claim ($2,000, $5,000, $8,000 or market value on the Absolute Warranty), and a claims line that pays the repairer directly. Consumer Affairs Victoria notes the warranty is part of the trader's contract and does not transfer if you sell the car privately before it expires. An Integrity warranty transfers to a private buyer within 7 days of sale with the transfer form, a condition report and a $62.50 fee. Two warranties can't overlap, so cover bought during the statutory warranty starts the day after it ends.

A VIC example

A 2017 Hyundai i30 with 95,000 km bought from a Dandenong trader gets the 3-month, 5,000 km statutory warranty. It is Category 1 and qualifies for every Absolute level; Level 2 (cap $5,000) for 3 years is $1,478, Level 3 (cap $8,000) is $1,782. Victoria requires a roadworthy certificate for transfer whether the sale is dealer or private, but a roadworthy is a safety check, not a mechanical guarantee, and gives you no warranty on a private sale.
